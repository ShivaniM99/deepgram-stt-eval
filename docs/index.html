Is Deepgram good enough for a pharmacy refill line?

A pharmacy wants a phone line where patients call to refill their medicines. Before switching it on, somebody has to check whether the speech recognition is good enough, and how you would know if it stopped being good enough. I tested it on 648 recordings. Here is what happened.

Hear it go wrong The code One-page summary

648 recordings · 67 minutes of audio · 4 qualities of phone line · total cost to run $0.93 · by Shivani Madan

The short version

The good news. It hears prescription numbers perfectly. 188 of them, not one mistake. And it never makes things up: played silence, hold music and keypad beeps, it stayed quiet every single time. Some systems invent whole sentences out of hold music.

The problem. Medicine names that sound alike get swapped for each other. Not garbled into nonsense, but swapped for a different real medicine, which is far worse, because the result looks perfectly normal and nobody downstream notices.

The surprise. You can give the system a list of medicine names to listen out for. Giving it all of them helps. Giving it half of them, which is what a pharmacy would naturally do by listing its best sellers, made the medicines left off the list get worse, and start coming back as the names of the ones on it.

Neither problem means switching vendors. Both are about how the phone system is built around the speech recognition.

Hear it go wrong

All of these are computer-generated voices, so anyone can recreate them from the code. Press play, then read what came back.

1. The same recording. One setting changed. A different medicine.

Nothing about the audio changes between these two results. The only difference is whether the system was given a list of medicine names to listen for.

what was said
I'm calling about cyclosporine 100 milligrams. My date of birth is August nineteenth, nineteen forty one.
no word list
I'm calling about cyclosporine one hundred milligrams. My date of birth is 08/19/1941.
half a word list
I'm calling about cycloserine one hundred milligrams. My date of birth is 08/19/1941.

Cyclosporine is an anti-rejection drug for transplant patients. Cycloserine treats tuberculosis. Both are real, and both get dispensed. The sentence reads perfectly, so nothing flags it. Adding a half-finished word list caused this. With no list, the same recording came out right.

2. The same words, once in studio quality and once down a phone line

Same speaker, same sentence. The second version has been put through a normal telephone connection.

studio quality
I'd like a refill on amiodarone two hundred milligrams. Prescription number 3562773.
phone quality
I'd like a refill on Ami order on two hundred milligrams. Prescription number 3562773.
+ half a word list
I'd like a refill on amlodipine. On two hundred milligrams. Prescription number 3562773.

“Ami order on” is obvious gibberish, and a pharmacist would spot it immediately. “Amlodipine” is a blood-pressure tablet, and nobody spots that. Notice the prescription number survived all three versions.

3. A recording with nobody speaking at all

Hold music. A real phone line is full of this: music, dial tone, keypad beeps, background hiss.

what it typed
(nothing at all)

Nothing, on all 24 no-speech recordings, under every setting. This matters more than it sounds. A system that invents a sentence out of hold music creates a prescription request with no patient behind it, and there is nobody to correct it.

The three findings

The checklist, and whether it passed

I wrote down what “good enough” has to mean before looking at the results, with a reason for every number. The pharmacy would set its own. The test runs automatically and fails loudly if a future update makes any of this worse.

What was checkedResultNeeded
Swapped one real medicine for anotherneverneverPASS
Got the medicine name right (phone quality)94%98%FAIL
Got the prescription number right100%95%PASS
Made something up when nobody spokeneverneverPASS
Got the date of birth right97%90%PASS
Made ordinary speech worsenonoPASS
Time from caller stopping to text appearing2.0 s2.5 sPASS
Requests arriving in pieces88%10%FAIL

What would fix it

  • Have it read the medicine name back and wait for a yes. The 94% is only acceptable if a mistake gets caught in the conversation. Without that step, roughly one call in sixteen carries a wrong medicine name into the pharmacy’s queue.
  • Wait for the whole sentence before doing anything. Collect the pieces and treat them as one request, rather than acting on the first piece that arrives.
  • Use the complete list of medicines, or none at all. Never a partial one.
  • Send uncertain medicine names to a human instead of guessing.

What this test cannot tell you

  • No real pharmacy calls were used. Those are private medical records. The real-voice recordings here are 16 clips in one person’s voice, which is enough to prove a problem exists but not enough to say how big it is across all callers.
  • I was wrong twice about the computer voices. I assumed they would make the results look better than reality. They made word accuracy look worse and the cut-off problem look smaller. That is the main reason the real recordings mattered.
  • Timings were measured from one laptop on one internet connection, so they include the time spent travelling to the servers and back.
  • Only single sentences were tested. No back-and-forth conversation, no interrupting.

The medicine pairs are taken from a published list of names that pharmacists really do mix up, kept by the Institute for Safe Medication Practices. Every recording on this page was generated by computer; nothing here is a real patient or a real call.
